Top 10 Interview Questions and How to Answer Them

Published January 2026  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  8 min read

Job interviews can feel nerve-wracking, but most interviewers ask a similar set of core questions. If you prepare thoughtful answers in advance, you walk in with real confidence instead of improvising under pressure. Below are ten of the most common interview questions, with guidance on how to answer each one, plus extra questions to expect for remote jobs.

One note before we start: do not memorise a script word for word. Aim to know your key points and your real examples so you can speak naturally. Interviewers can tell the difference between someone who is reciting and someone who is thinking.

1. "Tell me about yourself."

This is not an invitation to tell your life story. Give a short professional summary, around one to two minutes: who you are, your relevant background, your key skills and why you are interested in this role. End by connecting your experience to the job. A useful pattern is present, past, future: what you do now, how you got here, and what you want to do next.

2. "Why do you want to work here?"

Research the company before the interview. Mention something specific, such as a product you use, the way they work, their mission or a recent project. The goal is to show that you chose this company on purpose, not that you are sending the same answer to everyone.

3. "What is your greatest strength?"

Pick one strength that is directly relevant to the role, and back it up with a real example. Instead of "I am a hard worker", say something like "I am good at explaining technical issues in plain language. In my last job I rewrote our help articles, and the number of repeat questions dropped."

4. "What is your greatest weakness?"

Be honest but strategic. Choose a real weakness that is not essential for the job, and explain what you are doing about it. For example: "I used to avoid public speaking, so I started presenting short updates in team meetings, and it is getting easier." Avoid "I work too hard", because interviewers hear it constantly and it sounds evasive.

5. "Tell me about a challenge you faced and how you handled it."

Use the STAR method: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Describe a real challenge briefly, explain what you were responsible for, what you did and what happened. Spend most of your time on the action and the result, and mention what you learned.

STAR method: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Use it for any behavioural question that starts with "Tell me about a time when...". Prepare three or four stories in advance, because one good story can answer several different questions.

6. "Where do you see yourself in five years?"

Show ambition that fits the company. You do not need a perfect plan. You need to show that you think about your career seriously and can see yourself growing in this area. For example, "I would like to grow into a senior support role where I can help train others and improve our processes."

7. "Why are you leaving your current job?"

Stay positive and professional. Never criticise your current or former employer. Focus on what you are moving toward, such as new challenges, growth or a better fit with your skills, instead of what you are running from. If you were laid off, say so simply and move on to what you learned and what you want next.

8. "What are your salary expectations?"

Research the market rate first, then give a range rather than a single number. It is fine to say, "I am flexible, but based on my research the range for this role is X to Y." If you are not ready to answer, you can ask about the budget for the role. Our guides on negotiating your salary and the complete salary negotiation guide cover this in more depth.

9. "Do you prefer working alone or in a team?"

The honest answer for most roles is both. Explain when each works best and give an example of each: a project you delivered on your own, and one where teamwork made the result better. This is also a good opportunity to mention how you stay in touch with others when working remotely.

10. "Do you have any questions for us?"

Always say yes. Good questions show interest and help you decide whether the job suits you. You could ask what a typical week looks like, what the biggest challenge in the role is, how success is measured in the first three months, or how the team communicates. Avoid asking about pay or leave as your very first question.

What If You Do Not Know the Answer?

Every candidate gets a question they did not prepare for. Stay calm, take a breath and say, "That is a good question, let me think for a moment." If you genuinely do not know, be honest and explain how you would find the answer. A thoughtful response such as "I have not worked with that tool, but I learn new software quickly. For example, I taught myself the ticket system in my last job within a week" is far better than guessing or bluffing.

Know the Interview Format

  • Phone or video screening: a short call with a recruiter to confirm your background and expectations.
  • Hiring manager interview: deeper questions about your skills, experience and how you work.
  • Skills task or test: a practical exercise, which in remote jobs is often written or done on your own time. Ask how long it should take, and be wary of anyone who asks for a lot of free work on their real project.
  • Panel or final round: a conversation with several team members, where cultural fit and communication matter.

How to Practise

Practise out loud, not just in your head. Record yourself answering three or four questions and watch it back, or ask a friend to play the interviewer. Time your answers, and cut anything that rambles. If you are applying from a non-English-speaking background, practise speaking clearly at a steady pace. Clear and calm is more impressive than fast.

Extra Questions to Expect for Remote Jobs

  • "How do you manage your time when no one is watching?" Describe your routine, how you plan your week and how you track tasks.
  • "Which remote tools have you used?" Name the ones you really know, such as Slack, Zoom, Notion or Trello, and say how you used them.
  • "How do you communicate with people in other time zones?" Mention clear written updates and giving people the context they need to act without a call.
  • "What would you do if you were stuck on a problem?" Show that you try to solve it first, then ask a clear, specific question instead of waiting.
  • "What does your workspace look like?" Be ready to describe a quiet space and a reliable internet connection, and mention a backup option.

Good Questions to Ask the Interviewer

  • How does the team usually communicate, and how often do you meet?
  • What would you like the person in this role to have achieved after three months?
  • What are the working hours or time zone overlap expectations?
  • What do you like most about working here?
  • What are the next steps in the hiring process?

Before the Interview

  • Reread the job listing and your resume so that you can match your answers to the role.
  • Prepare three or four STAR stories.
  • Test your camera, microphone and internet connection, and choose a quiet background.
  • Log in a few minutes early.

After the Interview

Send a short thank-you email within a day. Thank the interviewer, mention one specific thing you enjoyed discussing, and repeat that you are interested. If you do not hear back by the date they gave you, a polite follow-up is perfectly acceptable. Whatever the result, write down the questions you found hard so you are better prepared next time.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Not researching the company at all.
  • Giving long, unfocused answers. Keep most answers to about two minutes.
  • Speaking badly about former employers.
  • Claiming skills you cannot demonstrate.
  • Forgetting to prepare questions of your own.

Strong answers start with a strong application, so make sure your resume and cover letter are ready too.
