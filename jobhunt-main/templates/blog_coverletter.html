How to Write a Cover Letter That Actually Gets Read

Published February 2026  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  7 min read

Most cover letters are never read closely. They are too long, too generic or too dull, and a hiring manager can tell within two lines whether a letter was written for this job or copied from a template. A good one does the opposite: it is short, specific and makes it easy to say "this person understands what we need".

Here is a practical way to write a cover letter that works, with examples, a complete sample, and a few notes for people applying to remote and international jobs. The examples are invented to show the structure, so replace the details with your own real experience.

What a Cover Letter Is Actually For

Your resume lists what you have done. Your cover letter explains why it matters for this particular job. It is your chance to connect the dots for the reader, to show that you read the listing, and to let some personality come through in your writing. That last point is especially important for remote roles, where employers rely heavily on written communication and want to see that you can write clearly.

If an application says a cover letter is optional, writing one is usually still worth it for jobs you really want, because many applicants skip it.

Keep It Short: Three to Four Paragraphs

Hiring managers are busy. Aim for roughly 250 to 350 words, which fits on half a page to a bit more. A simple structure works well: an opening that shows why you want this role, a paragraph that proves you can do it, an optional paragraph on how you work, and a clear closing.

Paragraph 1: Open With Something Specific

Do not begin with "I am writing to apply for the position of...". Everyone does that, and the reader has already seen it from the applicant before you. Instead, start with something specific about the company or the work that made you want to apply.

Weak: "I am writing to apply for the Customer Support Specialist position advertised on your website."

Better: "When I read that your support team answers every ticket within one working day, I knew I wanted to apply. I have spent two years handling customer chat and email for an online store, and fast, friendly replies are what I enjoy most."

Paragraph 2: Prove You Can Do the Job

Choose two or three of your most relevant achievements and connect each one to something the job description asks for. Use numbers where you honestly have them, and keep the language plain. Show that you read the listing carefully by using some of its own words.

"Your listing mentions managing a high volume of customer emails. In my current role I answer around 60 emails a day and keep our first reply time under two hours. I also wrote five help-centre articles that reduced repeat questions about delivery times."

Optional Paragraph: Show How You Work Remotely

For a remote job, one short paragraph about how you work independently is very effective. Mention your time zone and availability, the tools you use, and an example of work you delivered without close supervision.

"I work from Lahore and can cover US East Coast afternoons. I use Slack, Zoom and Notion daily, and I am comfortable documenting my work so teammates in other time zones can follow it without waiting for me."

The Closing: Be Clear and Confident

End by saying what you would like to happen next, and make it easy for them to respond. Keep the tone warm and professional, and avoid pleading or over-formal phrases.

"I would be glad to discuss how I could help your support team. I am available for a call at your convenience and can be reached at [email]. Thank you for your time."

Key rule: Every cover letter must be customised. Change the company name, the specific role details and the achievements you highlight for every application. A generic letter gets ignored.

A Complete Sample Letter

Here is a full example, invented for illustration, so you can see how the pieces fit together.

Dear Ms. Khan,

When I read that your support team answers every ticket within one working day, I knew I wanted to apply for the Customer Support Specialist role. I have spent two years handling chat and email for an online store, and quick, friendly replies are the part of the job I enjoy most.

Your listing mentions managing a high volume of customer emails. In my current role I answer around 60 emails a day and keep our first reply time under two hours. I also wrote five help-centre articles that reduced repeat questions about delivery times, and I trained two new colleagues on our ticket system.

I work from Lahore and can cover US East Coast afternoons. I use Slack, Zoom and Notion every day and I am comfortable documenting my work so teammates in other time zones can follow it.

I would be glad to discuss how I could help your team. I am available for a call at your convenience and can be reached at [email]. Thank you for your time.

Kind regards,
[Your name]

If You Have No Direct Experience

Do not apologise for what you lack. Focus on what you can show: a project, a course with a finished piece of work, volunteer or family-business experience, and the specific skills the job needs. For example: "I have not held a full-time support role, but I managed customer messages for my family's online shop for a year, and I completed a customer service course where I wrote the replies for fifty practice tickets." Honesty combined with evidence reads far better than vague enthusiasm.

Format and Delivery

  • Address a named person if you can find the hiring manager's name. If not, "Dear Hiring Team" is perfectly fine.
  • Use the same font and header as your resume so the two look like a set.
  • Send a PDF unless the listing says otherwise, with a clear file name such as "FirstName-LastName-Cover-Letter.pdf".
  • If you apply by email, you can paste a shortened version into the email itself.

A Ten-Minute Tailoring Routine

  • Read the listing and underline the three things the employer cares about most.
  • Find one specific fact about the company, such as a product, a value or a recent announcement.
  • Match your two best examples to those three needs.
  • Change the opening and closing, check names and spelling, and save a new copy.

Email Subject Lines and Messages

If you apply by email, the subject line is the first thing the reader sees, so keep it clear and professional. Follow the instructions in the listing if it asks for a specific format. If it does not, something simple like "Application: Customer Support Specialist, Your Name" works well. In the body of the email, two or three short sentences are enough, followed by your attached resume and cover letter. Do not write "see attached" and nothing else, because that wastes the chance to make a first impression.

Adjusting the Letter for Different Roles

  • Creative and writing roles: let your voice show a little more, and link to your portfolio in the first or last paragraph.
  • Technical roles: name the specific tools, languages or projects that match the listing, and include a link to your work.
  • Customer-facing roles: show warmth and clarity in your writing, since the letter itself is a sample of how you communicate.
  • Admin and operations roles: highlight reliability, organisation and examples of tasks you handled accurately.

What to Avoid

  • Starting with "To Whom It May Concern" when a name is easy to find.
  • Repeating your whole resume in paragraph form.
  • Talking about what the company can do for you instead of what you offer.
  • Clichés such as "team player" and "hard worker" without proof.
  • Leaving another company's name in by mistake.
  • Skipping a final proofread. One typo can cost you the interview.
