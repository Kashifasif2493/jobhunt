How to Write a Resume That Gets You Hired in 2026

Published January 2026  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  8 min read

Your resume has one job: to get you a conversation. It does not need to tell your whole life story, and it will not get you hired on its own. What it has to do is make it easy for a recruiter to see, quickly, that you can do the work they need done. Recruiters often go through a large pile of applications, so the first read is usually fast. A clear, well-organised resume makes that fast read work in your favour.

Below is how I would build a resume from scratch in 2026, step by step, with examples you can adapt. It is written for people applying to remote and international jobs, including readers in Pakistan who often learn a CV format that does not match what employers abroad expect.

1. Choose a Simple Format

For most people, a reverse-chronological layout works best: your most recent role first, then earlier ones. Use a single column, a readable font such as Arial, Calibri or Georgia at 10 to 12 points, and clear section headings. Plain formatting is not boring. It is what lets both a human reader and an applicant tracking system understand your resume without guessing.

Keep it to one page if you have less than around ten years of experience. Two pages are fine for longer careers, as long as the second page is not padding. If you are cutting something, cut the oldest or least relevant item first.

2. Get the Header Right

Put your name, a professional email address, your city and country, and a link to your LinkedIn profile at the top. For remote applications it also helps to add a short line such as "Open to remote work, available for US/UK overlap hours" if that is true. It saves the recruiter a question.

A phone number is optional for some roles and expected for others, so follow what the listing asks for. Choose an email that looks like a name, not a nickname or a string of numbers. If your current address is not professional, create a new one before you start applying.

3. Write a Short, Specific Summary

Two or three sentences at the top should say who you are professionally, what you are strongest at, and what kind of role you want. Compare these two versions:

  • Weak: "Hardworking and motivated professional looking for a challenging opportunity."
  • Better: "Customer support specialist with three years of experience handling email and chat for an online retail brand. Comfortable with Zendesk and Shopify, and focused on resolving issues in the first reply."

The second version tells the reader exactly what you can do. The first could be pasted onto any resume in the world.

Tip: Read the job description and note the words it repeats. If the listing asks for "customer onboarding" three times, and you have done customer onboarding, use those words. Many companies use applicant tracking systems (ATS) that match resumes to listings, and a human reader also looks for familiar terms.

4. Turn Duties Into Achievements

Under each job, list the company, your title, the dates and three to five bullet points. The most common mistake is writing what you were responsible for. Employers care more about what changed because you were there.

A reliable pattern for each bullet is: action, what you worked on, and the result. For example:

  • Before: "Managed social media accounts."
  • After: "Planned and scheduled daily posts for three brand accounts, growing Instagram followers by 40% in six months."
  • Before: "Responsible for answering customer emails."
  • After: "Answered around 60 customer emails a day and kept first-response time under two hours."

Use real numbers where you have them. If you do not have exact figures, an honest estimate with the word "around" is better than a made-up statistic. Never invent results, because interviewers will ask about them.

What a Finished Experience Entry Looks Like

Here is a complete example so you can see how the pieces fit together. Notice how every line is short and carries a fact.

  • Customer Support Agent, BrightCart Online (Remote), March 2023 to Present
  • Handle email and live chat enquiries for an online retail store, resolving most issues in the first reply
  • Wrote five help-centre articles that reduced repeat questions about delivery times
  • Trained two new team members on the ticketing system and escalation process
  • Maintained customer satisfaction scores above the team average for six consecutive months

You can see the job title, employer, location type and dates in the first line, followed by bullets that start with strong verbs and describe outcomes. You can follow the same structure for any field, from design to accounting to teaching.

Resume or CV: Is There a Difference?

In the United States and for most international remote jobs, a resume is a short summary of your career, usually one or two pages. A CV (curriculum vitae) is longer and more detailed, and is mostly used in academic, medical and research settings, and in many countries outside the US as another word for resume. If a listing asks for a CV and does not say more, send a clear two-page resume. If it asks for a specific format, follow that format exactly. Our guide on how to make a professional CV goes deeper on this.

5. What If You Have Little or No Experience?

Many first-time applicants worry that an empty work history makes a resume impossible. It does not. You can build a strong resume from:

  • Projects you completed, such as a website, a portfolio, a research paper or a small business you helped run
  • Freelance or volunteer work, even if unpaid
  • Courses and certifications that are relevant to the job you want
  • Responsibilities from student societies, family businesses or community work

Place a "Projects" section above or beside education and describe each one the same way you would a job: what you did and what came out of it.

6. Build a Useful Skills Section

List the tools, software and languages you can genuinely use, grouped clearly, for example "Tools: Google Workspace, Notion, Canva" and "Languages: English (fluent), Urdu (native)". Include soft skills only if you can back them up with a bullet elsewhere on the resume. "Good communicator" means little on its own, but "Wrote weekly client updates for four accounts" proves it.

Do not list skills you could not talk about for two minutes in an interview. Interviewers pick the one you are weakest on.

7. Education and Certifications

Keep this short. Write your degree, institution and graduation year. If you are early in your career, you can add a line about relevant coursework or a final project. Certifications belong here too, but only the ones that relate to the job. A list of twenty unrelated certificates looks like filler.

8. Make It ATS-Friendly

You do not need tricks, you just need to avoid things that confuse software. Use standard section headings such as "Experience", "Education" and "Skills". Avoid tables, text boxes, icons and multi-column templates when you can, because some systems read them in a jumbled order. Save the file as a PDF unless the listing asks for Word, and use a clear file name such as "FirstName-LastName-Resume.pdf".

9. Resume Habits to Drop When Applying Internationally

Many CV templates used in South Asia include details that employers in the US, UK and Europe do not expect and, in some cases, prefer not to receive. For remote and international applications, consider leaving these out:

  • A passport-style photo
  • Father's name, marital status, religion, date of birth or national ID number
  • A long "Objective" paragraph about wanting to grow with a progressive organisation
  • "References available on request" (it is assumed)
  • A personal address in full, since your city and country are usually enough

Instead, use that space for the evidence that matters: results, tools and projects.

10. Tailor It in Ten Minutes

You do not have to rewrite your resume for every application. Keep one strong master version, then for each job spend ten minutes doing three things. First, adjust the summary so it speaks to this specific role. Second, move the two or three most relevant bullets to the top of each job. Third, check that the key tools and terms from the listing appear if you genuinely have them. That small effort usually does more than sending the same resume to fifty companies.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Spelling and grammar errors. Read it aloud and ask someone else to read it too.
  • Generic or unprofessional email addresses.
  • Using fancy graphics or skill-rating bars that ATS cannot read and that say nothing about your ability.
  • Listing every task from every job. Choose what is relevant.
  • Leaving unexplained gaps without a line of context when the gap is long.
  • Sending a resume with the wrong company name left over from a previous application.

Final Checklist Before You Send It

  • Is it one clear page, with plain formatting?
  • Does the summary say specifically what I do?
  • Does every recent job have achievements, not only duties?
  • Do the skills match the listing and the truth?
  • Have I checked every date, link and spelling?

A resume is never really finished, and that is fine. Improve it a little each time you apply, and pair it with a strong cover letter and good interview preparation so the whole application works together.
