How to Find Remote Jobs With No Experience in 2026

Published June 2026  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  8 min read

If you have never held a remote job, the hardest part is often not the application. It is finding listings that you actually have a chance of getting. Most beginners type "remote jobs" into a search box and scroll through thousands of loosely related results, many of which ask for years of experience. The people who find entry-level remote roles faster usually do not use better websites. They search in a smarter way.

This guide is about job search engines and search habits: what they are, how they differ from job boards, and how to use them to find remote work when you are starting out. If you want a list of boards to browse, read our guide to the best websites to find remote jobs. Here we focus on the search itself.

Job Search Engines vs Job Boards

A job board hosts listings that employers post on it directly. A job search engine collects listings from many sources, including company career pages and other boards, and shows them in one place. The advantage is coverage: you can see far more jobs with a single search. The disadvantage is that the same job may appear several times, and quality varies from listing to listing.

In practice, the line is blurry. Large sites often work as both. The useful point is this: when you use a search engine, you are responsible for filtering, so your search terms matter much more.

Search Engines Worth Knowing

  • Google's job search. When you search for a role on Google, a jobs panel often appears at the top of the results. It gathers listings from many sites and lets you filter by date posted, job type and sometimes location, which makes it a good first stop.
  • Indeed. One of the largest job search engines worldwide. It pulls from employers and many other sources, and offers filters for remote work and date posted.
  • LinkedIn Jobs. More than a search engine, because you can see who posted the job, who works at the company and whether you have connections there.
  • Glassdoor, Adzuna and Jooble. Additional aggregators that are useful for cross-checking listings and spotting jobs the others miss. Glassdoor also shows employee reviews for many companies.
  • WorldJobShunt. Our own listings page collects remote roles and links to the original posting, with filters for category and country.

You do not need to use all of them. Pick two or three and learn how their filters work.

Search by Role, Not by the Word "Remote"

The most common beginner mistake is searching for "remote jobs" with no role attached. That returns every kind of work and rewards the listings with the most aggressive titles. Instead, start with the job you want, such as "customer support", "content writer" or "bookkeeper", and then add a location rule such as "remote" or "work from home".

Also try the alternative words employers use. Depending on the company, the same job might be described as "remote", "work from home", "distributed team", "anywhere" or "telecommute". Running your search with two or three of these catches listings the others miss.

Use Search Tricks That Save Time

  • Quotation marks. Searching for "customer support" with quotation marks finds that exact phrase instead of pages that mention the two words separately.
  • Minus sign. On Google, putting a minus before a word removes results that contain it. For example, you can exclude words that you know lead to unwanted listings.
  • Date filters. Set the date filter to the past week. Fresh listings have fewer applicants and are more likely to still be open.
  • Job alerts. Save a search and have new results emailed to you, so you do not have to repeat the same search every day.
  • Search company career pages. Many employers host their openings on applicant tracking systems such as Greenhouse or Lever. Searching for a role together with the name of one of these systems and the word "remote" can reveal postings that do not appear on mainstream boards.

Write down the exact search phrases that give you good results, and reuse them. A small list of three or four reliable searches is worth more than starting from scratch each day.

Build a Search Step by Step

Here is a simple way to build a good search, using customer support as an example. Start broad, then narrow.

  • Step 1: Search for the role on its own, such as "customer support", and see what kinds of titles appear. You may find "customer success", "support specialist" or "helpdesk" used for similar work.
  • Step 2: Add the work arrangement, for example "customer support remote" and then "customer support work from home".
  • Step 3: Apply the date filter so that you only see postings from the last seven days.
  • Step 4: Add a detail that matters to you, such as a tool you know (Zendesk, Shopify) or a field (e-commerce, software).
  • Step 5: Save the search as an alert.

You end up with a short, relevant list instead of thousands of loosely related results, and you can repeat the same search with other job titles.

Use Search Results to See What Employers Want

Search results are useful beyond the jobs themselves. When you read ten listings for the same role, you start to see which skills and tools appear in almost every one. That is your learning list. If most customer support jobs mention a particular help desk tool or ask for experience writing clear emails, you know exactly what to practise.

Copy those repeated requirements into a note, and compare them honestly with your resume. Anything you can do but have not mentioned should be added, and anything you cannot yet do becomes a short, specific goal for the next few weeks. Our guide on writing a resume shows how to present these skills clearly.

Set Up Alerts That Do Not Flood Your Inbox

Alerts are one of the most helpful features of any search engine, but too many of them become noise. Keep two or three alerts for your best searches and make them specific. Choose daily summaries rather than instant notifications if your inbox is busy, and delete alerts that return mostly irrelevant jobs. Review your alerts once a month and adjust the keywords as you learn what works.

Search Terms That Surface Beginner Roles

Employers use particular words when they are happy to train someone. Add one of these to your role search and you will see a very different set of results:

  • "entry level", "junior" or "associate"
  • "trainee", "apprentice" or "internship" (some remote internships are paid)
  • "no experience required" or "will train"
  • "assistant", as in "virtual assistant" or "marketing assistant"

Treat "no experience required" with care, though. It is used by genuine employers and by scammers. A real one will still describe the work, the pay structure and the hiring process in specific detail.

When Every Listing Asks for Experience

Many beginners get discouraged because listings ask for "2 to 3 years of experience". In practice, that number is often a wish rather than a rule. If you meet most of the requirements and can show relevant work, such as a project, a freelance task or a course with a finished piece of work, it is still worth applying. The ones to skip are roles that require a specific licence or a long track record that you cannot replace with proof of skill.

Use your search results to see which skills appear most often, and build a small piece of work around them. A short sample, a portfolio page or a clear description of a project shows you can do the job, and it often counts for more than a long list of duties.

Filter Out the Noise

Search engines show everything, including jobs that look remote but are not. Check three things quickly before you click apply:

  • The location rule. "Remote" may mean "remote within one country". If you live elsewhere, you cannot be hired for it.
  • The date. If a job was posted many weeks ago, it may already be filled.
  • The source. Click through to the original posting. If it leads to the company's own site, that is a good sign. If it leads to a vague form that asks for personal documents, be careful.

If you are applying from Pakistan or another country outside the main hiring markets, the location rule is the filter that saves you the most time. Roles marked "worldwide" or "anywhere" are the best place to start, and our guide to getting a remote job with no experience explains how to prepare for them.

Check What You Find

Because search engines collect listings automatically, they can include scams and duplicates. Before you apply, look up the company, check that the job appears on its own website, and be suspicious of any offer that asks you to pay a fee or share bank details early. For a full checklist, read our guide on how to avoid remote job scams.

Make Your Profile Easy to Find

Searching works in both directions. Recruiters also search for candidates, usually on LinkedIn. Use the same words in your profile headline and skills that employers use in job titles, and keep your location and availability clear. Our guide on LinkedIn profile tips explains how to set this up.

A Simple Daily Routine

  • Spend ten minutes checking your saved searches and alerts.
  • Shortlist two or three roles that match your skills and read them fully.
  • Verify each employer before applying.
  • Tailor your resume and send one or two strong applications.
  • Write the job link and date in a tracker so you can follow up.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Searching only for "remote jobs" without a specific role.
  • Ignoring the location rule and applying to jobs you are not eligible for.
  • Applying to the same listing through three different sites.
  • Paying money for suspicious job offers.
  • Giving up after a handful of applications. Most searches take time.

Final Thoughts

Finding remote jobs is as much about searching well as it is about where you search. Choose a role, use a few good search engines, filter carefully and verify every employer. Your first remote job may take time, but every well-targeted application teaches you something and moves you closer to the goal.
