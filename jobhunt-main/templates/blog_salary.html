How to Negotiate Your Salary and Get What You Deserve

Published February 2026  ·  Updated November 2026  ·  8 min read

Most people never negotiate their salary, and the reason is usually not that they do not deserve more. It is that they do not know how to start the conversation, so they accept the first number and hope it is fair. A salary conversation does not have to be confrontational. Employers expect it, and a polite, well-prepared request is normal professional behaviour.

This guide gives you a step-by-step approach, scripts you can adapt, and a few extra points for people applying to remote and international jobs, where pay can work differently from a local office role.

Step 1: Research the Market Rate First

You cannot negotiate without knowing what the work is worth. Before any salary conversation, find out what people in your role, with your experience level and in your market, are paid. Useful starting points include:

  • Glassdoor, for salary information by company and role
  • LinkedIn's salary insights, where available
  • PayScale, for salary calculators
  • Indeed's salary pages, for a wide range of industries
  • The job listing itself, which sometimes states a pay range

Tip: Collect three or four data points and note the low, middle and high end. Aim for the upper part of the range you can justify with your skills and results. Never ask for a number you cannot explain.

Step 2: Know Your Numbers

Decide three figures before you talk to anyone: the number you would be delighted with, a realistic target, and the lowest offer you would accept. That last one is your floor. Having it written down stops you from agreeing to something under pressure that you will regret later. Think about total pay too, not only the base salary, including bonuses, leave, equipment and any benefits.

Step 3: Wait for the Right Moment

The strongest time to discuss money is after you have received a formal offer, because at that point the employer has already decided they want you. If an application form asks for your salary expectation earlier, give a range based on your research, or write that you are flexible and would like to discuss it once you know more about the role.

Step 4: Do Not Accept the First Offer on the Spot

Even if the offer sounds good, take a short pause. Say thank you, say you are excited, and ask for a day or two to review the details. This shows professionalism and gives you time to prepare a calm response instead of reacting.

Step 5: Use Scripts You Can Adapt

When they ask what you are looking for:

"Based on my research and experience, I am looking for something in the range of $X to $Y. Is that within the budget for this role?"

When you receive an offer and want to negotiate:

"Thank you so much for the offer. I am really excited about this opportunity. Based on my research and the experience I bring, I was hoping we could get closer to $X. Is there any flexibility there?"

If they ask what you earn at your current job:

"I would prefer to focus on the value I can bring to this role. Based on what the role involves, I am looking for $X to $Y."

If they say the salary is fixed:

"I understand. Are there other areas we could discuss, like a signing bonus, extra leave, or a pay review after six months?"

Step 6: Negotiate Beyond the Base Salary

If the base pay cannot move, other parts of the package often can. Consider asking for:

  • A signing bonus
  • More paid leave
  • Flexible hours or more remote days
  • An earlier performance and pay review
  • A learning or equipment budget
  • A better job title if it matches your responsibilities

If the Job Is Remote or International

Remote roles add a few questions that are worth asking early:

  • Is pay based on location? Some companies use one global salary band, while others adjust pay to the cost of living where you live. Ask how it works and whether the amount is the same for colleagues in the same role.
  • Are you an employee or a contractor? A contractor usually handles their own taxes and gets no paid leave or benefits, so a contractor rate should be higher than an equivalent employee salary to cover that.
  • Which currency, and how will you be paid? Fees and exchange rates affect what actually reaches you. Our guide on receiving international payments in Pakistan explains the main options.
  • What working hours are expected? If the job needs you online during another country's business hours, that is part of the deal and a fair thing to discuss.

Prepare Your Evidence

A negotiation is much easier when you can explain why you are worth the number you ask for. Before the conversation, write down three or four things you can point to: results you delivered in past work, skills that match what the role needs most, relevant tools you already know, and anything that reduces the employer's risk, such as proven remote work experience. Your request then sounds like a reasoned proposal and not just a wish.

An Example Conversation

Here is a short, invented example of how a calm negotiation can go. The employer has offered a figure that is a little below the candidate's target.

Employer: "We would like to offer you $X per month for this role."

Candidate: "Thank you, I am really happy to receive this offer. I have been looking at similar roles and my own results in my last position, and I was hoping for something closer to $Y. Is there room to move on that?"

Employer: "We can go up a little, but not all the way."

Candidate: "That helps. If we can meet at $Z, I would be glad to accept. Could you send the updated offer in writing?"

Notice that the candidate stays friendly, names a specific number, gives a reason and asks for the final terms in writing.

Asking for a Raise in Your Current Job

The same ideas work when you want a raise. Gather evidence of what you have achieved since your last review, check what the market pays for your role now, and ask for a short meeting with your manager rather than raising it casually. Open by saying you would like to discuss your compensation, present your results, and name the figure or range you have in mind. If the answer is not now, ask what you would need to achieve to get a raise, and when you can review it again. A clear target and date turn a polite refusal into a plan.

How to Handle Different Responses

  • They agree. Say thank you and ask for the updated offer in writing.
  • They offer part of what you asked. Weigh it against your target and floor. You can accept, or ask once more politely about a smaller improvement elsewhere in the package.
  • They say they need time. That is normal. Agree on when you will hear back.
  • They say no. Thank them, and decide calmly whether the offer still meets your floor.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Naming a number before you have researched the range.
  • Apologising for asking, or saying "I know this is a lot".
  • Making threats or ultimatums you do not intend to keep.
  • Negotiating only by message when a short call would be clearer, or the opposite, if you struggle to stay calm on calls.
  • Accepting verbally and forgetting to get the final terms in writing.

The Golden Rule

Be polite, prepared and collaborative. Negotiation is a professional conversation, not a fight, and employers tend to respect candidates who handle it well.
